ILLUSTRATIVE · NOT AN OFFER

Straight answers to the questions serious investors ask first. Figures come from model v3 at its base settings.

What flow rate per well does the plan need?

The model rates each producing well in megawatts, net at the plant fence: 2 (low), 5 (base), 13 (high, a gross source figure used as an upper bound). The base is an assumption, not a measured Cooper Basin result.

A screening calculation (brine exergy × 40–50% utilisation × 71–86% after plant parasitics) says 5 MWe net needs roughly 64–97 kg/s per producer at 215 °C (104–158 kg/s at 169 °C).

So the base case needs several times the best sustained circulation achieved in this basin. At 1.4 MWe net per producer the 5 MW build needs 10 wells and A$431.0m; at 2 MWe, 8 wells and A$389.7m. A site flow, temperature and injectivity test decides this, which is why the first tranche is a reservoir test.

Who buys the electricity, and at what price?

Australia: nobody outside. The power runs behind the meter into Archegon's own hall; tenants pay a colocation rate that bundles space and power. We now price the power system as its own business: its levelised cost is A$280–383/MWh at 6–10% (A$330 at 8%), against SA wholesale of A$88/MWh. At 8% the power system absorbs about 124% of the hall's colo revenue. The premium for firm, carbon-free, fast-to-connect power has to be proven with tenant letters of intent.

There is no power-only fallback on the islanded Australian site: without a grid connection an unsold megawatt earns nothing. If a connection were built (cost not yet priced), exporting at SA wholesale would earn about A$4.8m a year.

New Zealand: Archegon buys power from an existing geothermal generator. The model uses NZ$105/MWh, which is one week of spot price. PUBLIC PROXY The ASX Otahuhu baseload futures strip settled at NZ$126.51/MWh for 2027 and NZ$133.86 for 2028 (ASX Energy, about 1 Oct 2026), and Contact Energy averaged NZ$140/MWh on power sold in FY2026 (report). At the 2027 strip the NZ route returns 4.5% instead of 5.2%. A hedge is not a physical PPA; a generator quote is still needed.

Is it firm power?

Not yet. The plant is sized so annual energy matches the load: 6.94 MW net nameplate × 0.90 capacity factor = 6.25 MW average. That is not firm. With one of the two producers out, 5.0 MW remains, enough for 4.0 MW of IT (three of four modules). The 10% of hours the capacity factor leaves uncovered is about 876 hours a year. A third doublet for N-1 adds A$41.25m; batteries, standby generation and a black-start source are needed. PUBLIC PROXY At public cost benchmarks (CSIRO GenCost 2025–26: 2-hour battery A$503/kWh, gas reciprocating engines A$2,022/kW; GenCost) a 2-hour grid-forming battery plus standby engines for the full 6.25 MW load cost about A$23.7m including contingency. Black start is assumed to come from those two; no separate public figure exists. These are utility-scale benchmarks, not quotes, and are not in the headline raise. Module 4 is offered as interruptible until a firm design is quoted.

Evaporative cooling holds output but uses three to four times the water in an arid basin with protected artesian springs. The vendor derate curve is a seed deliverable.

What do the Fervo short-seller claims mean for Archegon?

On 6 October 2026 short seller Morpheus Research, which holds a short position, published allegations against Fervo Energy, the leading US enhanced geothermal (EGS) developer. It alleged faster thermal decline at Project Red, about 30% fluid loss against the under 1% needed, 58 or more Yellow-level induced-seismicity events at Cape Station, and altered data. These are allegations, not findings. Fervo's published data show Project Red stable for over 500 days before a 2.5°F drop in the last 100 days, which it says was expected for the pilot's tight well spacing. In August 2026 Fervo published a positive independent review of its seismicity protocol. We have not found a point-by-point rebuttal. The shares fell well below the IPO price, and at least one US law firm has opened an investigation.

For us these are the same risks our plan already says are unproven: reservoir flow, decline, fluid loss and seismicity. No one has yet run an EGS reservoir in the Cooper Basin for years. Habanero ran for 160 days in 2013. We now set public pass/fail limits for the A$69.2m reservoir test:

An independent view, and its limits. On 16 October 2026 Tidal Wave Technologies, an independent consultancy, published a blog (link) countering the claim of a hidden 90°F drop in produced-fluid temperature at Project Red. Its author argues the figure comes from a monthly average of wellhead readings that includes cold readings taken while flow was throttled and then stopped in March 2024, so it is not a reservoir cooling event. This is an independent blog, not a statement from Fervo, and Fervo has not yet answered Morpheus point by point (checked 19 October 2026). The blog also accepts that a large cooling event is visible in March 2024 and that Fervo did not explain it. We treat both sides as unverified until the raw time series, with shut-ins flagged, is published. That is why our Gate 1 data-release test asks for exactly that.

What it does to returns. The wells can deliver 3.06 MW more than the plant uses. That margin absorbs decline: every 1% fall in reservoir temperature costs about 2.7% of net power, so up to about 1.0 °C a year all four modules stay lit for 25 years (Decline tab). What can we learn from Eavor's Geretsried project?

Eavor is a closed-loop developer, a different technology from the fractured-reservoir (EGS) design we model, but its first commercial project is a useful test of how plans meet execution. Per ThinkGeoEnergy (16 October 2026, report): Eavor announced the start of electricity supply at Geretsried, Germany, before the end of 2025. A few months later it disclosed gross output of only 0.5 to 2.0 MWe, with practically no power delivered to the grid. It is now withdrawing both drilling rigs to stop the cost of holding them on site, and the second loop is on hold while it negotiates a firm heat offtake agreement. The local district-heating company, which had signed in 2024 for 81,200 MWh a year of heat, said it was disappointed and will reassess its options. Eavor says Geretsried remains a priority and that the first loop validated many technical aspects of the concept.

What we take from it. First power is not the same as delivered power. Our plan therefore energises modules one at a time against measured output (Gate 2 sizes the plant to measured flow, not to a design number), tests the reservoir for 90–180 days before any plant is bought (Gate 1), and requires a priced offtake before the hall is built (G4). Standby cost for rigs held on site while a gate is pending is not priced in the model (RATE_MISSING). Geretsried is a different technology, heat-led rather than compute-led, and a single case; it does not show that our design would fail, only that these gates exist for good reason.

How do we protect output if the reservoir cools?

Every EGS reservoir cools as it is produced. Up to about 1.0 °C a year the existing well margin covers it. Above that we costed three ways to keep all four modules lit for 25 years (Headroom tab): (a) drill extra doublets at the start, (b) drill make-up doublets when the monitored trend calls for them, and (c) build fewer modules from day one. A doublet costs two wells plus stimulation (A$8.25m a well, half the well cost; EIA 2016) plus 25% contingency, and a make-up campaign pays rig mobilisation again. The ORC is not resized. What does it return?

Once built and full, the 5 MW reference earns about A$10.06m a year of contribution (revenue less plant O&M and team): 2.9% of the capital. Twenty-five years of it does not repay the capital. A 10% contribution yield would need about US$467/kW/month, 2.4× the benchmark. PUBLIC PROXY Adding the proxy-costed firm-power package and a 36-month runway (Fervo Cape Station's drilling-to-first-power span) lifts the capital to A$375.1m and cuts the yield to 2.7%. If a tenant instead paid the structure of a recently signed Texas hosting contract (US$150/kW/month plus US$0.13/kWh for energy; AZIO AI, 9 Oct 2026), contribution would be about A$18.2m, or 4.8% of that capital. That contract is grid-connected and in Texas, so this is an upper case, not a forecast. We state this openly: the Australian route is an option on a reservoir, not a yield asset, until a measured resource and a priced tenant change the numbers.

Bonded satellite. Many terminals can be joined with bonding routers. We size this at 50 Mbps per terminal at busy hour (Telstra), with bonding keeping 52% of the combined speed (Peplink user test). Each terminal needs its own priority plan, A$806 a month for 2 TB, plus A$38 per extra 50 GB (WhistleOut, Oct 2026). Terminal hardware is A$2,499 (Canstar), and each bonding router costs US$39,999, one at the site and one at the far end, for every 18 terminals (list price; Peplink). Three limits apply. Every user in a ground cell shares a licensed ceiling of about 20 Gbps (licence figures). The operator says extra terminals do not add speed linearly. Most of the cost is priority data. So bonding can just about carry a training-style load, at about A$26m a year, but it cannot carry inference at any terminal count.

Future satellite. A satellite gateway gives up to 10 Gbps for US$1.25m upfront plus US$75,000 per Gbps per month (2024 price). The first next-generation satellites, each rated at 1 Tbps, reached orbit on 28 September 2026 (SpaceX). They are not yet in service, and no per-cell capacity or price has been published. We show this as an option, not a plan.

Fibre. Moomba is 66 km from Innamincka in a straight line (source). We assume a route 1.2 times longer, built at A$125,000 per km, the middle of a published A$50,000–200,000 range for remote sites (SpaceSGP). A 2011 government program cost about A$41,667 per km (Treasury). Onward capacity to Adelaide is priced at the regulator's regional rate (ACCC, 2016). Third-party access to Moomba's network is not confirmed. If it is refused, fibre to Lyndhurst costs about A$55m. Revenue is capped by what the link can carry: GPU-hours beyond the link's capacity cannot be sold. For inference serving, only fibre carries the load; bonded terminals carry about 7% and a gateway about 4%, so those options never repay. Even on fibre, inference never repays at flat prices once onward capacity is priced at the regulated rate. Fibre is the base case. For training-style work with flat GPU prices, Australia returns 4.1% a year over 25 years with fibre; a future gateway costs about A$12.6m a year, and bonded satellite never repays. In New Zealand, a short fibre lateral is assumed, and the return is 8.5%.

Why Australia first, when New Zealand looks better?

On the model's own numbers New Zealand returns 5.2% of capital against Australia's 2.9%, with no drilling risk. Australia's case is ownership of a reservoir, which is an option with a probability and a payoff. Both routes stay open until a geothermal principal and a generator path exist.

What do "net", "gross", "average" and "firm" mean here?

Gross: generator output. Parasitic: what the plant uses itself (injection pumps, feed pumps, condenser fans). Net: gross minus parasitic, delivered to the site switchboard. Every per-well figure is net. Average: net nameplate × capacity factor. Firm: guaranteed every hour with one element out.
